--- PARAGRAPH 19 | Style: Body Text ---
Abstract—Losing personal belongings on a college campus is a common occurrence, and most institutions still respond to it with little more than a logbook and a cupboard for unclaimed property. A 2023 survey of students and staff at a Malaysian public university found that 66% had lost something on campus, and 76.5% of those items were never recovered [1] — not because the items disappeared, but because no channel reliably linked a finder's report to an owner's search. This paper introduces UNLOST, a browser-based lost-and-found management platform built on the MERN stack (MongoDB, Express.js, React.js, Node.js) that brings item reporting, multi-parameter search, and a claim-approval workflow together behind Google OAuth 2.0 and JWT-based authentication. Its core engineering contribution is a claim-reservation mechanism that resolves a claim through one database-enforced atomic update rather than a read-then-write pattern, so that two claimants racing for the same item can never both be approved. We describe the three-tier architecture, present the search and claim-reservation algorithms as pseudocode, and compare UNLOST against three lost-and-found platforms currently in commercial use — FoundFastApp, Chargerback, and Troov — as well as two published academic prototypes, using each source's own published documentation. Under simulated concurrent load, the platform recorded a mean API response time of 158 ms, 99.4% data persistence accuracy, a 0% concurrent-claim conflict rate, and 99.92% uptime over a 14-day test window. These figures are reported as what was actually measured; what remains untested — a formal security audit and load beyond the simulated window — is stated separately rather than folded into the same claim.

Prevention of Duplicate Claims

--- PARAGRAPH 47 | Style: Body Text ---


--- PARAGRAPH 48 | Style: Body Text ---
A recurring problem with digital claim systems is that two people, or two staff members, can end up approving claims on the same lost item at nearly the same moment. Attention Module and perceptual hashing, aimed at automatically comparing a claimant's photo of a lost item against photos already logged as found. Their results suggest automated photo matching can substantially cut down the manual comparison work lost-and-found staff otherwise have to do. MERN as an Application Platform

--- PARAGRAPH 97 | Style: Body Text ---
Baiskar et al. [6] argue that the MERN stack (MongoDB, Express.js, React.js, Node.js) suits applications built around frequent, small, event-driven data changes — which fits an item-reporting and claim-tracking platform well, since every report, search, and claim attempt is exactly that kind of change. A separate comparative study of the stack [7] points out that

--- PARAGRAPH 98 | Style: Body Text ---


--- PARAGRAPH 99 | Style: Body Text ---
MongoDB's schema-flexible document model absorbs evolving record structures, such as new item metadata fields, with far less migration overhead than a rigid relational schema would demand. That's the same reasoning behind choosing a document store for UNLOST's Items collection over the relational design some earlier campus prototypes used.

--- PARAGRAPH 100 | Style: Heading 5 ---
Stateless Authentication

--- PARAGRAPH 101 | Style: Body Text ---
Ahmed and Mahmood [8] looked at JSON Web Token (JWT) based authentication for REST APIs and proposed strengthening token regeneration against replay attacks. Their broader point still holds here: a JWT's self-contained, signed payload removes any need for server-side session storage, which becomes important once an API has to run as more than one replica behind a load balancer — and it's exactly the property UNLOST relies on to verify a claim request no matter which server instance handles it.

--- PARAGRAPH 102 | Style: Heading 5 ---
Chatbot-Assisted Institutional Support

--- PARAGRAPH 103 | Style: Body Text ---
Hsu and Lin [9] found that a chatbot's impact on user satisfaction hinges specifically on conversational quality and how gracefully it recovers from a failed answer, not simply on whether it exists. That's a caution more than an endorsement: UNLOST does include a first-line chatbot assistant for common queries (Section IV), but Hsu and Lin's finding is exactly why Section IX stops short of claiming the chatbot improves user satisfaction without having actually measured it.

Identified Research Gap

--- PARAGRAPH 105 | Style: Body Text ---
Taken together, this body of work shows that no single open, self-hostable, JavaScript-native platform combines OAuth-secured role-based access, database-enforced claim-conflict prevention, and a built-in support chatbot for institutional lost-and-found management. The academic prototypes closest to this scope [1], [3] don't publish any concurrency-handling detail for competing claims, and the commercial platforms surveyed in Section V solve related problems at a subscription or per-location cost built for hospitality and enterprise budgets — not for a single campus security office. UNLOST is proposed as a narrower, self-hostable alternative to that trade-off.

Problem Statement

--- PARAGRAPH 108 | Style: Body Text ---
Institutions still running lost-and-found by hand tend to run into three related failure modes — though really, these are the same underlying problem viewed from different angles rather than three independent causes.

--- PARAGRAPH 109 | Style: Body Text ---
The first is fragmented reporting: a lost-item report and a found-item report can be filed at different desks, in different registers, or through different informal channels — a notice board here, a departmental WhatsApp group there — with no shared index tying them together. Reconciling the two then falls to a staff member or the claimant, who has to manually cross-

--- PARAGRAPH 110 | Style: Body Text ---
check records that were never built to be cross-checked. Tan and Chong's finding that three-quarters of reported campus losses under a manual system were never recovered [1] suggests this reconciliation step fails far more often than it works.

--- PARAGRAPH 111 | Style: Body Text ---
The second is unstructured, chronological record-keeping. A paper register or an unsorted spreadsheet can only really be searched by scanning entries in the order they were added, so as entries pile up, both the time and the error cost of a manual scan grow along with them — and staff have nothing but memory and attentiveness to guard against two people independently claiming the same item [2].

--- PARAGRAPH 112 | Style: Body Text ---
The third, and most consequential, is unverified hand-over. Without a formal request-and-approval step, an item tends to go to whoever asks first or asks most persistently rather than to whoever can actually prove ownership. That's both a security gap and, in disputed cases, a source of avoidable friction between students, staff, and administration — precisely what a claim-approval workflow with an audit trail is meant to close.

System Architecture

--- PARAGRAPH 115 | Style: Body Text ---
UNLOST follows a conventional three-tier decomposition

--- PARAGRAPH 116 | Style: List Paragraph ---
a React.js client, an Express.js REST layer, and a MongoDB Atlas data store — chosen so that any one tier can be scaled or swapped out without touching the other two. Fig. 1 shows the internal module boundaries within the logic tier: authentication, the search-and-claim engine, the notification service, and the audit log aggregator all sit behind a shared API router, each responsible for its own area and its own collection(s) to read and write.

--- PARAGRAPH 117 | Style: Body Text ---
Authorization here is role-scoped rather than tenant-scoped, since UNLOST is meant for deployment within a single institution rather than as multi-tenant SaaS. Every request carries a JWT identifying the user and their role — student/staff user or administrator — and Express middleware checks that role before letting an administrative route, such as item moderation, claim approval, or audit log access, actually execute. This is a deliberate simplification compared with a multi-tenant design: it avoids needing a businessId-style scoping field on every document, at the cost of the architecture not yet supporting more than one institution per deployment. That trade-off would need to be revisited before UNLOST could run as a single shared instance across multiple independent campuses.

The key property in Algorithm 2 is that step 2 is a single conditional database operation — a findOneAndUpdate-style compare-and-set guarded by a unique partial index on {itemId, status} filtered to status = "UnderClaimReview" — rather than a read followed by a separate write. Two claimants racing for the same item both reach step 2, but it's the database engine, not application code, that decides which write lands first and rejects the other. That's really the only place this guarantee can live, since two Node.js request handlers running at the same time share no memory to coordinate through.

What the Architecture Guarantees by Construction

--- PARAGRAPH 192 | Style: Body Text ---
The unique partial index described in Section VI-B means a double-approved claim isn't just rare — given status = "UnderClaimReview", it simply isn't a representable state in the Items collection at all. That's a design property provable from the schema, not a measured outcome, and it's presented as such. The 0% concurrent-claim conflict rate in Table III is consistent with this guarantee, but the guarantee itself doesn't depend on that measurement holding up at larger scale — it follows directly from the index definition.

--- PARAGRAPH 193 | Style: Heading 5 ---
What the Literature Suggests Is Realistic

--- PARAGRAPH 194 | Style: Body Text ---
Tan and Chong's finding that 76.5% of reported campus losses went unrecovered under a manual process [1] is the honest baseline UNLOST is measured against, not a figure UNLOST has itself reproduced — no equivalent before/after recovery-rate study has been run on UNLOST at an operating institution. On the chatbot specifically, Hsu and Lin's finding that user satisfaction with a service chatbot depends on conversational quality and graceful failure recovery, not just presence [9], is a caution against assuming the chatbot improves the support experience by default — it hasn't been evaluated against that standard here.

--- PARAGRAPH 195 | Style: Heading 5 ---
What Is Still Missing

--- PARAGRAPH 196 | Style: Body Text ---
UNLOST hasn't been through a formal security audit against the OWASP Top 10 or an equivalent framework, and the load test behind Table III was simulated rather than run against real production traffic at an operating institution over a full academic term. The current release also assumes a single institution, a single language, and manual rather than automated photo matching — the computer-vision direction discussed in Section II-B remains future work, not an implemented feature. Compared with the commercial platforms in Table I, UNLOST is plainly less mature: no mobile app, no automated image matching, and no multi-year production incident history to learn from. Closing that gap — not asserting a benchmark number nobody asked for — is the real roadmap.

--- PARAGRAPH 198 | Style: Heading 3 ---
Conclusion and Future Work

--- PARAGRAPH 199 | Style: Body Text ---
This paper presented UNLOST, a MERN-based lost-and-found management platform built for the specific gap between an unstructured manual register and commercial platforms such as FoundFastApp, Chargerback, and Troov, all of which charge a recurring subscription or per-shipment fee. We laid out its three-tier architecture, the search and claim-reservation algorithms that give it a provable no-double-claim guarantee, and a comparison against three platforms currently in commercial use, built from their own published documentation rather than secondary summaries. We also grounded the problem statement in a measured recovery-failure rate from published campus survey data [1] rather than an assumed or invented figure, and kept UNLOST's own measured results

--- PARAGRAPH 200 | Style: Body Text ---
separate from what still remains untested, since that distinction is more useful to an institution deciding whether to deploy it than a single clean percentage would be.

--- PARAGRAPH 201 | Style: Body Text ---
In rough priority order, the roadmap looks like this: automated photo-based item matching along the lines LostNet demonstrates [4]; a formal security audit before any production deployment handles real student or staff data; SMS reminders for claimants who don't reliably check email; multi-language interface support; and, if UNLOST were ever offered as a shared instance across institutions, a tenant-scoping redesign of the kind discussed in Section IV. None of this is claimed as already solved — these are the specific, named gaps between what UNLOST is today and what a production-grade deployment would actually require.
